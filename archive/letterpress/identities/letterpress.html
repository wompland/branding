Sample correspondence

Small publishing apps, each printed in its own ink.

Dear reader,

WompLand is a family of small apps for publishing on your own server. blogWomp keeps a blog. wikiWomp keeps a wiki. Each one does a single job, shares accounts and content with the others, and leaves your words in files you can carry anywhere.

We set the family like a box of good stationery: one house monogram, and a card for every Womp, cut from the same stock and engraved in an ink of its own. The cards that come with this letter are the whole system. The blank one is for the next Womp.

Yours, from the WompLand press

One cartouche, one W, a new ink

Every Womp's monogram is the roman W with the Womp's own initial laid over it in italic, in name order, inside the same cartouche, printed in one ink nobody else in the family uses; the name follows in Literata, written as it is spelled. That's the whole rule: a new Womp needs a letter, an ink and its name, not a designer.

Inks on cotton, foils on black card

Light mode prints deep inks on cool cotton-white stock. Dark mode stamps pale foils on black card. Every pair below is read from the theme build's contrast report, so this page can't disagree with the gate. The standard is WCAG 2.2 AA, with AAA (7:1) for body text in both modes.

Two faces, kept apart

Bodoni Moda engraves the monograms and any display line 28px or larger. Literata sets everything people read. JetBrains Mono is for code only.

Keeping a commonplace book in public

By Ines Marlow (sample author) 12 November 2026 6 minute read

For years my commonplace book lived in a paper notebook with a cracked spine: quotations, recipes, a train timetable I liked the look of. When I moved it to the web I expected to lose the feel of it. I didn't, mostly because I kept the rules the notebook had taught me. Copy things out by hand, say where they came from, and never pretend the page is finished.

The web version is plainer than the notebook. Each entry is a short post with a source link and one line about why I kept it. There is no feed algorithm and no count of anything. People who want it subscribe to the RSS feed, and a few follow along from the fediverse, which is how the replies at the bottom of each page arrive.

A commonplace book is a conversation with the people you copied from,
and now they can answer.

The part I didn't expect is the answering. When someone links to an entry from their own site, a webmention shows up under the post. Last month the author of a book I'd quoted wrote back with the page number I'd gotten wrong. Setting it up took one line in the page head: 

<!-- in the <head> of every page -->
<link rel="webmention" href="https://blog.example/webmention">
<link rel="alternate" type="application/rss+xml" href="/feed.xml">

If you keep something similar, I'd like to read it. Send a link from your own site and it will turn up below, the way a letter turns up in a box: slowly, and addressed to you.

Backups

Contents
  1. What gets backed up
  2. Schedule
  3. Restoring

The home server keeps three copies of anything worth keeping: the live disk, a nightly snapshot on the second drive, and a weekly copy that leaves the house. The tool is Restic; the offsite end is described in Offsite copies.

What gets backed up

Everything under /srv, which holds the blog, this wiki, and the photo library, plus the database dumps written by the nightly job. Caches and the media transcode folder are skipped; they rebuild on their own.

A backup you haven't restored from is a hope, not a backup. The quarterly drill is on Restore drill (page not written yet).

Schedule

Snapshots run at 02:30 every night and keep 14 dailies, 8 weeklies and 12 monthlies. The offsite copy runs Sunday at 04:00.

Restoring

Mount the snapshot read-only, copy back what you need, and check file ownership before restarting anything. See Updates for the restart order.
